# Bank Valuation Assignment Workflow in India: Step-by-Step Guide for Valuers

From the outside, a bank valuation assignment looks like two steps: visit the property, send the report. Inside the office, it is not two steps at all.

A single assignment passes through many small handovers — from the branch to the office, from the office to whoever does the site visit, from the field back to whoever prepares the report, from the draft to the valuer, and finally to submission and storage. Each handover is a point where a detail can slip.

Most delays in a valuation office are not caused by poor valuation skill. They happen when these handovers are not tracked — when no one can say, at a given moment, where an assignment is and who holds it next.

This guide sets out a practical, repeatable workflow for a bank valuation assignment, stage by stage, in the way many Indian valuation offices run it.

A note before we start: this is not the official workflow of any particular bank. Practices vary by bank, by office, by location, and by property type. Treat it as a practical reference for valuers and valuation offices — something to adapt to how you already work, not a fixed rulebook.

## The stages at a glance

1. Receiving the assignment
2. Capturing the key details
3. Assigning and planning the site visit
4. Conducting the site inspection
5. Capturing site evidence
6. Returning evidence to the office
7. Entering valuation inputs
8. Preparing the report draft
9. Valuer review and correction
10. Final approval and sign-off
11. Submission and storage
12. Follow-up and retrieval

Let's take them in order.

## 1. Receiving the assignment

An assignment usually arrives from a bank branch — sometimes directly from a branch official, sometimes through an empanelment channel or an agency. It may come by WhatsApp, email, a phone call, or a portal.

However it arrives, the first discipline is simple: log every new assignment the same way, in the same place, every time. It also helps to give each assignment an internal reference or file name right away — a short, consistent way to identify it in every list, folder, and conversation that follows. An assignment that lives only in a chat message, with no reference of its own, is already at risk of being missed.

## 2. Capturing the key details

Before anything else moves, the office should capture the details the rest of the workflow depends on:

- An internal assignment reference or file name
- Bank and branch details
- Borrower name, and co-borrower if any
- Borrower (and co-borrower) contact number, plus an alternate number if available
- Property address, with a clear landmark and the locality, village, taluk, or district
- A map pin or GPS location, where it is useful
- Type of property, as far as it is known
- Expected timeline, if provided or internally set

For instance, if the borrower's number is noted wrongly or the landmark is left vague, the site visit can't be fixed and the engineer may reach the wrong lane — a delay created at the very first stage, before any valuation work has even begun.

Getting the contact details and location right at intake is the cheapest way to avoid the most common delays later.

## 3. Assigning and planning the site visit

Next, decide who owns the visit — the valuer, an assistant, or a site engineer — and assign it clearly, so one person is responsible for the next step.

Then comes borrower coordination, which is often the real work of this stage. Someone calls the contact, fixes a date and time that suits them, and confirms the exact location. Site visits get rescheduled often — the borrower isn't available, the documents aren't ready, the weather doesn't cooperate. Each reschedule should update the assignment's status, so the office always knows the current plan rather than the original one.

Before going, prepare:

- Confirm the appointment, and re-confirm the location, landmark, and map pin if one was captured
- Note which documents the borrower can keep ready (this varies by case)
- Carry the basics: a measuring tape or laser, a camera or phone, and a notebook or site form for observations

A short pre-visit check like this prevents the most expensive mistake of all: a wasted trip.

## 4. Conducting the site inspection

At the property, the inspection is about recording the physical reality of the site as the valuer or site engineer sees it. This is the one stage that cannot be done from the office.

The person on site works through the property in a consistent order — the approach and access, the boundaries, the structure, the surroundings, and the internal condition where it can be seen. They check what is on the ground against what the documents say, and note anything that does not match. Measurements are taken where needed. Whatever is observed is written down on the spot, in a site form or notes, rather than left to memory for later. A visit documented carefully here saves a second trip and makes every stage that follows easier.

## 5. Capturing site evidence

Good evidence makes it easier for the office and valuer to review the file later. The aim is to capture enough that the report can be prepared — and explained, if a query comes — without going back to the site.

Depending on the property and office practice, useful evidence may include:

- **Photos** — frontage, the approach and access, surroundings, boundaries, the structure, and the internal condition where accessible
- **Document photos** — clear images of the documents produced or available at the site (whatever is shown, not a fixed list)
- **Observations** — construction type, approximate age, number of floors, finishing, and any visible issues
- **Location and access** — the road and access (width, type, whether motorable), and the character of the locality
- **Boundaries and condition** — boundaries as seen on site versus the documents, occupancy (self-occupied, rented, or vacant), and overall condition

The key discipline is to tie every photo and note to this specific assignment — by its internal reference — not to drop them into a general phone gallery where they mix with dozens of other properties.

## 6. Returning evidence to the office

Once the visit is done, the evidence has to reach whoever prepares the report. This handover is one of the most common leak points in a valuation office.

When photos sit in a personal WhatsApp chat and notes stay on one person's phone, the report-preparer ends up chasing details — and sometimes works with incomplete or mismatched evidence. Attaching all the evidence to the right assignment, under its reference, removes that friction.

## 7. Entering valuation inputs

With the evidence in hand, the valuer decides the inputs the valuation rests on. Depending on the property, this may include the land area and rate, the building details and construction rate, the age and the depreciation applied, the usable or built-up area, and any adjustments or reference points the valuer considers relevant.

These are professional decisions, and they belong to the valuer. The workflow's job is not to make them, but to make sure they are recorded clearly against the assignment — so the staff member preparing the draft is working from the valuer's actual inputs rather than guessing, and so the basis of the valuation is easy to revisit later.

## 8. Preparing the report draft

In most offices, a staff member prepares the report draft using the office's own template, usually in Word. They bring together three things: the details captured at intake, the site evidence from the visit, and the valuation inputs decided by the valuer.

This is assembly work, and it goes faster when the inputs are clear. When notes are vague or evidence is missing, the draft stalls and the staff member has to go back to the valuer or the site person with questions. A complete file means a clean first draft. And it is worth remembering what a draft is: a starting point, not the final report, until the valuer has reviewed it.

## 9. Valuer review and correction

This is the quality gate of the whole workflow, and it rests entirely on the valuer's judgment.

The valuer reads the draft against the evidence — checking that the details are right, the inputs and reasoning hold, the photos and documents match the property, the measurements and areas are consistent, and nothing has been carried over from another file by mistake. Corrections are made here, sometimes more than once. The earlier stages exist precisely to make this review possible: to put a complete, accurate file in front of the valuer, so the review is about judgment, not about hunting for missing pieces.

## 10. Final approval and sign-off

Once the valuer is satisfied, the report is finalised and signed off. At this point the assignment moves from "in progress" to "ready."

A clear sign-off step matters because, without it, a draft can quietly go out without the final check it was meant to get.

## 11. Submission and storage

The finished report is submitted as per the office's normal process — printed, emailed, uploaded, or sent through a portal, depending on the case.

Storage deserves as much attention as submission, though it rarely gets it. The final report, along with its evidence and the inputs behind it, should be stored against the assignment reference, in a place the office can actually navigate — not in a folder of near-identical file names that no one can search months later. The few minutes spent storing a file properly are repaid the first time someone has to find it again.

## 12. Follow-up and retrieval

An assignment isn't always over at submission. A branch may come back with a clarification, or a query may arrive weeks later. The same property — or a similar one nearby — may come up again in future.

How quickly the office can retrieve the old report and its evidence depends entirely on how it was stored. When a branch asks for a clarification later, a file you can pull up in seconds is worth far more than one you have to hunt for.

## Why status tracking matters at every stage

Across all twelve stages, one question should always have a clear answer: where is this assignment right now, and who holds it next?

When that answer lives only in someone's head, a handover can fail silently — a visit isn't rescheduled in the records, a draft sits unreviewed, a report waits — and no one notices until the branch or borrower follows up. A clear status at each stage turns those silent gaps into visible ones the office can act on early.

## Starting simple: a workflow before any software

You don't need software to run this workflow. You need consistency.

A single shared sheet can hold it together if it has fixed columns for what matters: assignment reference and date, bank and branch, borrower and contact, address and landmark, expected timeline, visit owner, site visit status, evidence received, inputs done, draft status, review status, submitted, and file location. Pair that with one habit — naming every file and photo folder by the assignment reference — and even a small office can run an orderly workflow long before it adopts any tool. The point is not the tool. It is defining the stages, and giving each one a clear status and a clear owner.

## Practical Checklist

**A per-assignment workflow checklist**

*At intake:*
- [ ] Created an internal assignment reference / file name
- [ ] Logged the assignment in one fixed place
- [ ] Captured bank and branch details
- [ ] Captured borrower and co-borrower, with contact and an alternate number if available
- [ ] Captured address, landmark, and locality / village / taluk / district (and a map pin if useful)
- [ ] Noted the expected timeline

*Before the visit:*
- [ ] Assigned one clear visit owner (valuer, assistant, or site engineer)
- [ ] Coordinated with the borrower and confirmed date, time, and exact location
- [ ] Updated the status if the visit was rescheduled
- [ ] Noted which documents the borrower can keep ready

*At the site:*
- [ ] Captured frontage, approach/access, surroundings, boundaries, structure, and condition
- [ ] Photographed the documents produced or available
- [ ] Recorded observations and measurements on the spot

*After the visit:*
- [ ] All evidence attached to the assignment reference
- [ ] Valuation inputs recorded against the assignment
- [ ] Draft prepared from details, evidence, and inputs
- [ ] Valuer reviewed and corrected the draft
- [ ] Report signed off
- [ ] Submitted and stored against the reference, where it can be found later

## FAQs

**Is this the official bank valuation workflow?**
There is no single official version that applies to every bank. Practices vary by bank, office, location, and property type. This is a practical workflow you can adapt to how you already work.

**Who should do the site visit — the valuer or a site engineer?**
It depends on the size of the office and the property. What matters is that one person clearly owns the visit. Regardless of who inspects, the valuation inputs and the final review stay with the valuer.

**What causes most delays in a valuation office?**
Untracked handovers. When details and evidence are scattered across WhatsApp and memory, stages stall without anyone noticing. A wrong contact number or an unclear landmark at intake is a common trigger.

**Do small offices need software to run this?**
Not to begin with. A consistent shared sheet, plus the habit of naming every file and folder by the assignment reference, can run an orderly workflow. Software becomes useful as the volume of assignments grows.

**Does a workflow — or software — prepare the valuation for me?**
No. It organises details, evidence, and status. The valuation itself — the inputs, the reasoning, and the sign-off — stays entirely with the valuer.
