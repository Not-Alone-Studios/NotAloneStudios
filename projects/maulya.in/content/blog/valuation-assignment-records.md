A valuation office does not need more paperwork. It needs records that can answer basic questions quickly.

What is this assignment? Where is it right now? Who is handling it? What did we observe at site? Has the report been reviewed? Was it submitted, and when? Where is the final file?

If your office can answer those questions in under a minute for any live or past assignment, your record keeping is working. If answering them means calling three people and scrolling a WhatsApp group, it is not — no matter how many folders you have.

Good records are not about volume. They are about being able to answer the obvious questions without a search party.

## What "better records" actually means

There is a common misunderstanding that better record keeping means a bigger, stricter system — more registers, more columns, more rules. In practice, heavier systems tend to collapse faster, because nobody has time to maintain them.

Better records mean the opposite: keeping the *minimum useful record* for every assignment, consistently. Not everything. Just the few things that let the office track, prepare, review, submit, and later retrieve the work.

The goal is a record that is light enough to actually maintain and complete enough to actually rely on.

## The minimum useful record for each assignment

For most valuation offices, a useful assignment record captures a small, predictable set of fields:

- **Assignment reference** — one reference that everything else hangs off
- **Received date** — when the assignment came in
- **Bank and branch** — who it is for
- **Borrower and co-borrower** — whose property it is
- **Property address and location** — what is being valued, and where
- **Contact details** — for the site visit and follow-up
- **Assigned person** — who in the office is handling it
- **Site visit date and status** — done, pending, or scheduled
- **Documents received and pending** — what came in, what is still awaited
- **Photos and evidence location** — where the site evidence actually sits
- **Valuation inputs and notes** — areas, rates, observations
- **Report draft status** — not started, in progress, ready for review
- **Valuer review status** — reviewed and approved, or pending
- **Final report submitted date and mode** — when, and how, it went to the bank
- **Final file location** — where the submitted file lives

Written out, that looks like a long list. In practice, most of it is one line per assignment. The point is not to fill every field perfectly. It is to have a *place* for each, so nothing important lives only in someone's head.

## Records should follow the workflow, not sit separately

Here is where many offices go wrong. They keep a tracker in one place and the actual work in another. The tracker says "report in progress," but the report, photos, and documents are scattered across folders, chats, and phones with no connection between them.

When the record and the work drift apart, the record slowly becomes fiction. It says one thing; the files say another.

Records work best when they follow the assignment as it moves — intake, site visit, evidence, valuation inputs, report preparation, review, submission, storage. Each stage updates the same record. The record is not a separate diary kept after the fact. It is the running state of the assignment itself.

## What good records quietly make easier

When the record actually reflects the work, a lot of daily friction disappears:

**Daily tracking.** You can see, at a glance, what is pending, what is at site, what is waiting for review, and what is ready to submit — without asking anyone.

**Report preparation.** The person preparing the report already has the inputs, photos, and documents in one place, instead of chasing them.

**Valuer review.** The valuer can see what was observed and what supports the numbers before signing off.

**Branch follow-up.** When a branch asks for status, the answer is on the screen, not in someone's memory.

**Old file retrieval.** Months later, the same record points to the final report and its evidence.

**Staff handover.** When someone is on leave or leaves the office, their assignments do not become mysteries. The record carries the knowledge they would otherwise have carried alone.

## A simple system for small offices

Even in a small office with one valuer, one office staff member, and one field person, the record should not live only in one person's memory. People take leave, phones change, and conversations get forgotten. If your office is small and you want something that will actually stick, keep it to a handful of rules:

**One tracker.** A single place — even a well-structured sheet — where every assignment is listed and updated. Not three sheets and two notebooks.

**One assignment reference.** Every assignment gets one reference, used on the folder, the report, and the tracker. This one habit removes most retrieval pain on its own.

**One file space per assignment.** Report, photos, documents, and notes go into one space tied to that reference.

**Fixed status names.** Agree on a small set of status words — for example, Received, At Site, In Preparation, Under Review, Submitted, Closed — and use only those. Mixed wording makes a tracker unreadable.

**A daily update habit.** Five minutes at the end of the day to move statuses forward beats a heroic monthly clean-up that never happens.

None of this requires new software. It requires agreement and consistency.

## What not to do

A few habits quietly undo good record keeping:

**Keeping records only in WhatsApp.** Chat is for coordination, not memory. Anything that needs to be found later should not live only in a group.

**Using inconsistent file names.** When every person names files their own way, the office loses the ability to find anything by pattern.

**Keeping evidence in personal phone galleries.** Photos on personal phones leave with the phone — and the person.

**Relying on memory for final submission.** "I think it went last week" is not a record. The submission date and mode should be written down.

**Marking a report complete before it is stored.** A report is not done when it is sent — it is done when it is sent *and* stored where the office can find it.

## The simple test for a good record

A good assignment record should be able to answer five plain questions about any file:

- What is this assignment?
- Where is it now?
- Who handled it?
- What evidence supports it?
- Where is the final report?

If your records answer those without a search, you are not just keeping files. You are running an office that can find its own work.

## FAQs

**1. What is the minimum a valuation office should record per assignment?**
A small, predictable set: assignment reference, received date, bank and branch, borrower, property and location, assigned person, site visit status, documents, evidence location, valuation inputs, draft and review status, and the final submitted date, mode, and location. Most of it is one line each — the value is in having a place for all of it.

**2. Should assignment records be separate from the actual files?**
It works far better when they are not. A record kept separately from the work tends to drift into fiction. Records hold up when they follow the workflow and stay connected to the same file space as the report, photos, and documents.

**3. Can a small valuation office manage with just a spreadsheet?**
Yes, if it is disciplined: one tracker, one assignment reference, fixed status names, one file space per assignment, and a daily update habit. Software usually earns its place as volume grows and more people start handling files at once.

**4. Why do WhatsApp-only records cause problems later?**
Chat is built for coordination, not retrieval. It is hard to search months later, evidence shared there often stays on personal phones, and there is no reliable record of final submission. It is fine for moving work along — not for being the office's memory.

**5. What status names should we use?**
The exact words matter less than consistency. A small fixed set such as Received, At Site, In Preparation, Under Review, Submitted, and Closed is usually enough. The discipline is using only those, every time, so the tracker stays readable.
