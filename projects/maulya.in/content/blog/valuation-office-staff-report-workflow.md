# From Site Photos to Final Report: Office Staff Workflow for Valuation Firms

In many valuation firms, the report does not fail because the typist typed slowly. It fails because the office staff were asked to prepare a report from incomplete evidence.

This is worth saying clearly, because office staff are often treated as the least important link in the chain — "just the person who types the report." In reality, they sit at the busiest junction in the whole office. Field evidence, valuer inputs, the report format, the review, the final dispatch — all of it passes through them.

When the material reaching them is clean, their work is faster and the valuer's review is more controlled. When it isn't, report preparation turns into a guessing exercise — and guessing is exactly what you don't want anywhere near a valuation report.

This article lays out how office staff can move from site evidence and valuer inputs to a clean draft, what to check before they start, and where the firm's biggest workflow risks actually sit.

## Why office staff workflow matters more than it seems

A valuation report is an assembly job before it's a typing job. Someone has to bring together the property and borrower details, the right photographs, the site observations, the measurements, and the valuer's inputs — in the office's format — into one coherent document.

That assembly is the office staff's real work. The typing is the easy part. The hard part is doing the assembly without filling gaps by assumption.

A firm that treats this role as pure data entry tends to get reports that look fine and quietly carry errors — a wrong photo, a guessed figure, a detail that was never actually confirmed. A firm that treats it as a checking-and-assembly role catches those gaps before the valuer ever sees the draft.

## What office staff receive

Depending on the office's process, the material that lands with the report preparer usually includes:

- **Assignment details** — the reference, the bank or branch, the type of valuation
- **Borrower, bank, and property details** — names, the property address, the case particulars
- **Site visit photos** — exterior, approach, interior where available, surroundings
- **Document images** — whatever was collected or shown at the site
- **Site observations** — what the field person noted on the ground
- **Measurements** — dimensions taken on site, as per the office's process
- **Valuer inputs** — the rates, the basis, and the figures the valuer provides
- **The report template or format** — as per the office's normal process

Some of this comes from the field, some from the valuer, and some from the office's own records. The preparer's first job is not to start typing — it's to check that all of this is actually present and usable.

## Valuation Office Staff Report Workflow: Before Drafting, During Drafting, and After Approval

The rest of this comes down to three phases — what to verify before drafting, how to draft without guessing, and what happens after the valuer approves. Each phase has its own discipline, and most stuck reports trace back to one of them being skipped.

## What office staff should check before drafting

This is the step that separates a clean report from a stuck one. Before writing a single line, the preparer should run through a short check:

- Is the **assignment reference** clear, so everything attaches to the right file?
- Are the **bank, branch, borrower, and property details** complete?
- Are the **photos grouped correctly**, and do they clearly belong to this property?
- Are the **document images readable** — can the survey number, area, and measurements actually be read?
- Are the **site observations** available, or is there nothing recorded from the field?
- Have the **valuation inputs** been provided by the valuer, or are they still awaited?
- Are **pending items** clearly marked, so they're tracked rather than forgotten?

If something here is missing, the right move is to flag it now — not to start drafting around the hole and hope it fills itself. A gap caught before drafting costs a question. The same gap caught during the valuer's review costs a full round-trip.

## The drafting workflow

Once the material checks out, the drafting itself is fairly straightforward:

1. **Prepare the report structure** in the office's format for this type of assignment.
2. **Insert the property and borrower details** from the assignment.
3. **Select the relevant photos** — the clearest, most representative images for each part of the property.
4. **Include the site observations** as recorded by the field person.
5. **Enter the valuation inputs exactly as provided by the valuer** — not adjusted, not estimated.
6. **Mark anything unclear for the valuer** instead of guessing it.

Points five and six are the heart of safe report preparation. The preparer assembles and presents; the valuer decides. Where the preparer is unsure, the answer is a marked query for the valuer — never a quiet guess.

## What office staff should never do

Some lines should not be crossed, regardless of deadline pressure or how routine the file looks:

- **Never assume missing valuation inputs.** If a rate or figure wasn't provided, it gets asked for — not invented.
- **Never make valuation judgment.** Deciding value, rates, or basis is the valuer's role and no one else's.
- **Never silently fill uncertain data.** An unconfirmed detail should be flagged, not quietly entered as if it were confirmed.
- **Never mix photos from other files.** Every image must belong to this property and this assignment.
- **Never send a draft out as final without valuer review.** The draft is a draft until the valuer has reviewed and approved it.

None of these are about competence. A skilled, fast preparer can still cross one of these lines under pressure — and that's exactly when a firm gets a report it later regrets. The discipline is to treat these as fixed, not negotiable.

## The valuer review and correction loop

Once the draft is ready, it goes to the valuer for review. This is not a formality. It's where judgment is applied and where the preparer's flagged queries get answered.

A clean draft makes this loop short. The valuer reviews, resolves the marked queries, corrects what needs correcting, and the preparer updates accordingly. A messy draft — one full of guesses and unflagged gaps — turns this into several rounds of back-and-forth, which is where days quietly disappear.

The healthiest version of this loop has the preparer making the valuer's review easy: queries clearly marked, nothing hidden, uncertainties surfaced rather than buried. That's not extra work — it's what makes the review fast.

## Final report, after approval

Only after the valuer has reviewed and approved the report does it become final. At that point it's prepared for dispatch to the bank or branch, and — just as importantly — stored where it can be found again.

That last part is easy to neglect under deadline pressure, but it matters. A report that's dispatched and then effectively lost is a problem the next time the same property, the same borrower, or the same branch comes up. Storing the final report and its evidence in a retrievable way is part of finishing the job, not an optional extra.

## Why office staff need a clean status system

Across all of this runs one quiet requirement: the preparer needs to know the status of every file at a glance.

Which assignments are waiting on field evidence. Which are waiting on valuer inputs. Which are drafted and waiting on review. Which are approved and waiting on dispatch. Which have a pending item that's blocking everything else.

When this status lives only in someone's head, or scattered across WhatsApp and a notebook, files fall through the cracks — and the branch's follow-up call becomes the status system by default. When status is visible and current, the preparer works in order of what's actually ready, and nothing sits forgotten.

The office staff workflow, done well, doesn't force anyone to guess. It gives the preparer enough verified material to assemble a clean draft, and the valuer a draft that's easy to review and sign. That's the whole job — and it's a lot more than typing.

## An office staff report-preparation checklist

**Before drafting (the gate)**
- [ ] Assignment reference is clear and everything attaches to the right file
- [ ] Bank, branch, borrower, and property details are complete
- [ ] Photos are grouped correctly and clearly belong to this property
- [ ] Document images are readable
- [ ] Site observations are available
- [ ] Valuation inputs have been provided by the valuer
- [ ] Pending items are marked and tracked

**While drafting**
- [ ] Report built in the office's format for this assignment type
- [ ] Property and borrower details inserted from the assignment
- [ ] Clearest, most representative photos selected
- [ ] Site observations included as recorded
- [ ] Valuation inputs entered exactly as the valuer provided them
- [ ] Anything unclear marked as a query for the valuer — not guessed

**Never**
- [ ] Assume missing valuation inputs
- [ ] Make valuation judgment
- [ ] Silently fill uncertain data
- [ ] Mix photos from other files
- [ ] Send a draft as final without valuer review

**After approval**
- [ ] Report finalised only after valuer review and approval
- [ ] Final report prepared for dispatch
- [ ] Report and evidence stored where they can be retrieved later

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are valuation office staff just data-entry operators?**
No. They assemble field evidence, valuer inputs, and the report format into a coherent draft, and they check that material before drafting. The typing is the easy part; the assembly and checking is the real work.

**What should office staff do when valuation inputs are missing?**
Ask the valuer for them. Missing rates or figures are never to be assumed or estimated by the preparer — that crosses into valuation judgment, which isn't their role.

**What should office staff check before they start drafting?**
Whether the assignment reference is clear, the case details are complete, the photos belong to this property, the document images are readable, site observations exist, valuer inputs are provided, and pending items are marked. A gap caught here costs a question; the same gap caught at review costs a round-trip.

**Can office staff send a report directly to the bank after preparing it?**
Not as final. A draft remains a draft until the valuer has reviewed and approved it. Dispatch happens after approval, following the office's process.

**Why does storing the final report matter?**
Because the same property, borrower, or branch often comes up again. A final report that's dispatched and then lost creates avoidable work later; storing it retrievably is part of finishing the assignment.
