In a valuation office, files rarely get lost while someone is actively working on them. They get lost while moving from one person to another.

A site engineer measuring a plot is focused on the site. A staff member preparing a report is focused on the draft. You, as the valuer, are focused when you sit down to apply judgment and sign off. The problem is almost never the work itself. The problem is the gap between two people — the moment the file changes hands and nobody is quite sure whose turn it is.

That gap is where bank valuation work quietly slips.

## The file doesn't fail in the work. It fails in the handoff.

Picture a normal Tuesday. Your site engineer is on the road with three visits across two talukas. Your office staff is preparing two reports and waiting on a tax receipt for a third. You are switching between a site of your own, a branch officer who just called for a status update, and a draft that needs your review before it goes out.

Every person here is busy. Nobody is sitting idle. And yet one file can sit untouched for two days — not because anyone stopped working, but because nobody realised it had become their job.

This is why handover problems are so hard to catch. They never show up as a clean, obvious mistake. They show up as a delay nobody can fully explain when the branch finally asks where the report is.

## Where a bank valuation file actually changes hands

A single assignment passes through more hands than most offices stop to count. The usual chain looks something like this:

1. **Branch to office** — the assignment arrives, often by email, sometimes by a call or a message from a branch officer.
2. **Office to site engineer** — someone has to physically go and see the property.
3. **Site engineer back to office** — measurements, photos, and site notes come back.
4. **Office staff to valuer** — a draft is assembled and put in front of the valuer for judgment.
5. **Valuer back to staff** — corrections and changes go back.
6. **Final approval to office** — the report is signed off and dispatched.
7. **Submitted report to records** — the final report is stored for future retrieval.

Seven handovers for one file. Each is a place where something can be dropped — and in a busy office, something often is.

## What goes wrong at each step

It is worth being honest about the failure points, because they repeat across offices.

At the **branch-to-office** stage, the assignment often arrives incomplete: a property address with no landmark, no borrower contact number, or no clarity on which branch officer to revert to. The work starts on the back foot.

At the **office-to-site-engineer** stage, the site engineer is sometimes sent with only a half-address and a vague instruction. The site engineer reaches the area, can't locate the property, and calls the office — which now has to call the borrower, who isn't picking up.

At the **site-engineer-back-to-office** stage, photos land in a WhatsApp group with no labels. Two weeks and forty assignments later, nobody can tell which photo belongs to which property, and the measurement slip is still in the engineer's bag.

At the **office-staff-to-valuer** stage, the valuer receives a file that looks ready but isn't. Halfway through, a question surfaces — was the latest property tax receipt received? — and the file slides back into limbo.

At the **valuer-back-to-staff** stage, corrections are given on a call. The staff member remembers four of the five changes. The fifth surfaces only when the branch points it out.

And at the **storage** stage, the report is sent and everyone moves on. Six months later, when the branch asks for a copy, the search begins.

None of these are competence problems. They are handover problems.

## What every handover should carry

A clean handover is not a long form. It is a small, consistent set of details that travel with the file every time it changes hands. At minimum:

- **Assignment reference** — so the file is identifiable, not "the Vijayanagar one"
- **Current status** — where the file actually is right now
- **Responsible person** — who is holding it at this moment
- **Next action** — what specifically needs to happen next
- **Pending items** — what is still missing or awaited
- **Deadline or timeline** — when it is expected to move
- **Evidence and documents** — photos, measurements, and papers kept with the file
- **Notes and corrections** — anything the next person must know

When these travel together, the next person doesn't have to reconstruct the file from memory or chase three people to understand what they've been handed.

## "I sent it on WhatsApp" is not a handover

This is worth saying plainly. A photo or a message sent on WhatsApp, floating loose in a chat that also contains forty other assignments and someone's lunch plan, is not a handover. It is a message that happens to exist somewhere.

A handover means the information is tied to a specific assignment, and the next person knows it is now their turn and what they are expected to do. If the file isn't connected to an assignment reference and a clear next action, it hasn't really been handed over — it has just been forwarded.

The same applies to verbal handovers. "I told them about it" is not a system. Memory is not a record. The moment work depends on someone recalling an instruction from three days ago, the office has already lost a little control.

## Move the file without moving the judgment

There is one line that matters more than any tool or format: handover should let staff support the workflow without taking over the valuer's professional judgment.

Office staff can move a file along, chase pending documents, organise photos, assemble a draft, and flag what's missing. That is real, valuable work, and it keeps the office running. But the valuation itself — the view on rate, depreciation, realizable value, and the final figure — remains the valuer's responsibility. A good handover system makes this separation cleaner, not blurrier. Staff handle the movement of the file; the valuer owns what the file concludes.

This distinction protects both sides. Staff are not asked to make calls they aren't qualified to make, and the valuer isn't pulled into chasing every small pending item.

## How a small office can start

You do not need software to fix handovers. You need a habit.

Start with a simple handover note that travels with every assignment — even a shared sheet or a single-page format will do. Every time the file moves, the person handing it over fills four lines: what is this file, where is it now, who owns the next step, and what is still pending.

That is it. Four honest lines, updated at every handover. Many offices that adopt even this much find that the "where is my report" calls drop, simply because someone can always answer the question without a thirty-minute investigation.

As volume grows, this is exactly the kind of discipline that becomes hard to maintain on paper — and that is the point at which a connected workflow starts to earn its place.

## Practical Checklist: A Clean Handover Note

Every time a file changes hands, the handover should carry:

- [ ] Assignment reference number
- [ ] Property address with a locatable landmark
- [ ] Borrower / contact name and number (and alternate contact, if any)
- [ ] Current status of the file
- [ ] Person currently responsible
- [ ] The specific next action required
- [ ] Pending items (documents, photos, inputs still awaited)
- [ ] Expected timeline or deadline
- [ ] Evidence and documents attached or linked to the file
- [ ] Notes or corrections the next person must know

**Busy-day short version:** What is this file? Where is it now? Who owns the next step? What is still pending?

## FAQs

**1. What is a valuation office handover workflow?**
It's the way a bank valuation assignment moves between the people who handle it — branch to office, office to site engineer, engineer back to office, staff to valuer, and so on — along with the details that travel with the file at each step, so the next person knows the status, what's pending, and what to do next.

**2. Why do valuation files get delayed even when everyone is working?**
Because delays often happen in the gaps between people, not inside anyone's work. A file can sit untouched simply because the handover was unclear and nobody realised it had become their turn to act.

**3. Should office staff handle the valuation itself?**
No. Office staff can move the file, chase pending items, organise evidence, and assemble drafts, but the valuation judgment — rate, depreciation, final figure — remains the valuer's professional responsibility. A good handover keeps that separation clear.

**4. Is a WhatsApp message enough for a handover?**
Not on its own. If the message isn't tied to a specific assignment and a clear next action, it's just a forwarded item floating in a busy chat. A handover means the next person knows the file is now theirs and what they need to do with it.

**5. How can a small office improve handovers without software?**
Begin with a simple handover note that travels with every file. At each handover, record what the file is, where it is, who owns the next step, and what's pending. Even this much reduces repeated calls and "where is the report" questions.
