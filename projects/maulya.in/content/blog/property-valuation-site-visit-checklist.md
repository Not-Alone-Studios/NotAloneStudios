**A site visit is not complete just because someone reached the property and took a few photos.**

It is complete when the person who went there comes back with enough information, measurements, images, and observations that the report can be prepared and reviewed without a single "can you check that again?" call. A practical **property valuation site visit checklist** exists for exactly that reason: to make sure the field person leaves the property with enough to finish the file, not to leave the office chasing details for a week.

That is the real test of a site visit. Not whether someone went. Whether the office can now finish the file.

### Why site visit quality quietly decides report quality

Almost everything after the site visit can be corrected from the desk. The typing, the area cross-check, the photo selection, the valuer's review and sign-off — all of it can be redone if needed.

The site visit cannot. Once the field person leaves the property, whatever they missed is missing. And in a far-off location — a plot in a taluk, a house two villages out, a site reachable only by a rough approach road — going back is not a small thing. It can cost a full working day.

So the cost of a weak site visit is not just a weak report. It is repeated phone calls, a file that sits and waits, a branch asking where the report is, and sometimes a second visit nobody had time for.

A checklist does not replace field judgment. What it does is make sure the obvious things are not forgotten under field pressure — heat, hurry, a talkative owner, three visits the same day. That alone prevents most second visits.

### Before leaving the office: preparation decides half the visit

Many "incomplete" site visits were actually decided before the person left. They reached the property under-prepared and improvised.

Pull the **assignment reference** first, so everything from the visit can be tied back to one file later. Confirm the **contact person and a working number** — a surprising number of field people reach the locality and then cannot reach anyone to open the gate. Get the **full address with a landmark**, not just a pin.

This matters far more in semi-urban and rural assignments than people expect. For a plot in a taluk or a village, the map pin can drop near a temple or a bus stop several hundred metres from the actual site, and without a working contact number the field person ends up asking shopkeepers for directions and burning an hour before the visit even starts.

Know the **property type before you go**. A vacant plot, a flat in an apartment, and an independent house need very different things measured and photographed. Walking in blind means you discover this on site and forget something.

Where possible, ask in advance **which documents will be available at the site** — sale deed, sketch, tax receipt, approved plan, where applicable. In many cases the documents are not ready at the site, which is fine, but knowing that in advance is better than being surprised.

And carry the basics that get forgotten: a **measuring tape or laser, a charged phone, a notebook or a site form**, and the assignment details. The site form is the difference between writing things down and trying to remember six visits later.

### On reaching the site: confirm before you measure

The first job at the property is not measuring. It is confirming you are at the **correct property**.

Match the plot number on the gate or wall, check it against the sketch, and where there is any doubt, ask a neighbour. A wrong-property visit is the most expensive mistake in this whole process, and it usually happens because someone assumed instead of confirming.

Then note **how you actually reached the property** and the **approach road** — width and type as seen, whether it is a tar road, a kachha road, or a narrow lane. Look at the **surroundings**: residential, commercial, or mixed; what is nearby; any landmark worth recording.

Confirm the **boundaries as seen on site** — what is on each side, to the extent visible. Note the **occupancy**: self-occupied, tenant-occupied, vacant, locked, or under construction. And note the **construction stage and condition** as observed.

A common field reality: the house is **locked and the owner is not available**, so the interiors cannot be seen or photographed. That is fine — but it has to be *recorded clearly as a pending item*, so the report preparer knows the inside was not inspected rather than quietly assuming it was. A silent gap becomes a problem; a noted gap is just a follow-up.

### Measurements and observations: record, don't conclude

This is where a careful field person earns their value.

Capture the **land area as per the document and as observed or measured on site, where possible**, and where there is a difference, *note the difference* — do not resolve it on the spot and do not write a conclusion about it. Capture **built-up, plinth, or carpet area** where relevant, the **number of floors**, the **construction type** (for example RCC framed or load-bearing, as observed), and the **age and condition** as seen.

If something looks off — an extra floor, a structure that seems to sit beyond the boundary, an apparent deviation — **record it as an observation with a photo**. Do not write that it is unauthorised. Do not write that the title is bad. That is not a field call.

Keep this dividing line clear, because it keeps the whole file clean: **the site engineer or field person records what is observed or measured on site; the valuer remains responsible for interpretation, valuation judgment, and the final sign-off.** A field note describes. It does not decide.

### Photos and notes: the field person's handover

Photos are the office's only window into a property they never saw. At minimum the visit should bring back the core set — **frontage, approach road, surroundings, boundaries where visible, interiors where accessible, documents shown at site, and any visible issue.** Which photos actually help, how many is enough, and the common mistakes are covered in detail in the companion article on site photos, so this checklist keeps the photo step short on purpose and sends you there for the depth.

Just as important is the **note that goes back with the visit**: visit completed date and time, who was present, what was collected, what is still pending, and any follow-up needed. This is the handover.

A site visit should not end with photos sitting silently in a phone gallery. It should end with a clear handover to whoever prepares the report — so they know exactly what they have and what is missing before they start typing, and so that if the branch later asks for a clarification, the office can answer from a complete file rather than a half-remembered visit.

### A site visit that ends well

The purpose of a site visit checklist is not to make the visit mechanical. It is to make sure the office comes back with enough reliable information to prepare and review the report properly — without a second trip, and without a week of WhatsApp follow-ups.

## Practical checklist

**Before leaving the office**
- [ ] Assignment reference noted
- [ ] Contact person and a working phone number confirmed
- [ ] Full address with a landmark (not only a map pin)
- [ ] Property type known in advance (plot / flat / independent house / under construction)
- [ ] Documents likely available at site checked in advance, where applicable
- [ ] Measuring tape or laser, charged phone, notebook or site form

**On reaching the site**
- [ ] Property identity confirmed (gate/wall number, sketch match, neighbour where needed)
- [ ] Approach road and access noted (width and type as seen)
- [ ] Locality and surroundings noted
- [ ] Boundaries as seen on site recorded (each side, where visible)
- [ ] Occupancy noted (self-occupied / tenant / vacant / locked / under construction)
- [ ] If locked / owner unavailable, interiors marked as a pending item
- [ ] Construction stage and condition observed

**Measurements and observations**
- [ ] Land area as per document vs as observed or measured on site, where possible (difference noted, not concluded)
- [ ] Built-up / plinth / carpet area where relevant
- [ ] Number of floors
- [ ] Construction type as observed
- [ ] Age and condition as observed
- [ ] Visible deviations or issues recorded as observations with photos (no legal conclusion; valuer interprets)

**Photos (see the site-photo article for the full set)**
- [ ] Core set captured: frontage, approach, surroundings, boundaries, interiors where accessible, documents, any visible issue

**Closing note to office**
- [ ] Visit completed date and time
- [ ] Who was present
- [ ] What was collected
- [ ] What is still pending
- [ ] Any follow-up required

## FAQs

**1. Who should use this site visit checklist — the valuer or the site engineer?**
Both, in their roles. A site engineer or junior who does the field visit uses it to make sure nothing is missed on the ground. The valuer uses the same structure to confirm the visit brought back enough to prepare and review the report. The field person records; the valuer interprets and signs off. The roles are different, but the information needed is the same.

**2. Is there one official site visit checklist that all banks follow?**
No. Requirements vary depending on the bank and the office's own process, and they can differ by property type and assignment. This is a practical working checklist, not an official format. Always follow the specific assignment instructions and your office's normal process.

**3. What should the field person do if the house is locked or a document is not available at the site?**
Record what *was* seen, note clearly that the interior was not inspected or that the document was not available, and flag it as pending in the closing note. The mistake is staying silent — the report preparer then assumes it exists and discovers the gap days later.

**4. If something looks like an unauthorised construction, should the site person write that in the notes?**
They should record it as an *observation* with a photo — for example, "structure appears to extend beyond the marked boundary on the rear side." They should not write a legal conclusion about whether it is authorised. That interpretation is the valuer's call, not a field judgment.

**5. How do you avoid a second site visit?**
Prepare before leaving, confirm property identity on arrival, and end the visit with a written handover that lists what is collected and what is pending. Most second visits happen not because the property was complicated, but because the first visit returned incomplete information that nobody noticed until report drafting.
