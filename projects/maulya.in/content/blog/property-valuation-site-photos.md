**A phone gallery full of photos is not the same as site evidence.**

Anyone can come back from a site with sixty photos. The question is whether those sixty photos let someone who never saw the property understand it — clearly, weeks later, when a question comes up.

That is what site photos really are. Not images for the file. **Evidence the office reads after the visit is over.**

### Why photos matter long after the site visit

The person who writes the report often did not go to the site. The person who reviews and signs the report almost never went. And the branch officer who asks a clarification three months later definitely was not there.

For all of them, the photo is the only window into that property. If the photo set is clear and complete, the report gets written smoothly and later queries are easy to answer. If it is a blurry, repetitive jumble, the file turns into a series of "send me that photo again" messages — and the property's memory slowly disappears.

This is why "did you take photos?" is the wrong question. The right question is "did you take photos someone else can actually use?"

### Many photos vs useful photos

These are not the same thing, and the difference shows up at the desk.

Eighty photos of the same wall is not evidence — it is one photo taken eighty times. A useful set is smaller and deliberate: it tells the property's story in sequence, from the approach, to the frontage, to the boundaries, to the inside, to whatever needs explaining.

A good rule of thumb: **before you leave, ask whether the photos would let a colleague who never visited describe this property.** If yes, you are done. If not, you are missing something, and it is far cheaper to take it now than to drive back.

### A practical bank valuation photo checklist, by group

Treat these as a working set, grouped so they are easier to remember on site — and adjusted to the property and the assignment, not a fixed legal list.

#### Anchor and context photos

These place the property and help identify which one it is.

- **Frontage** — the building or plot itself, clearly identifiable as the subject property. This is the anchor of the whole set.
- **Approach road and access** — how you reach the property, the road width and type as seen.
- **Surrounding locality** — the character of the area and any nearby landmark.
- **Landmark or approach reference** — a recognisable point that helps locate the property later.

For an **open plot**, the frontage and the boundary photos are doing most of the work, since there is no building to describe — so they need to be clear and unambiguous. For a **rural or taluk property**, the approach road is often the first thing a reviewer asks about: a narrow mud road reads very differently from a tar road, and the photo settles it without a phone call.

#### Property detail and condition photos

These describe what is actually there and what state it is in.

- **Boundary sides where visible** — each side, to the extent you can see it. Boundaries are among the most asked-about things later.
- **Building exterior** — all visible elevations, not just the front.
- **Internal rooms and areas where accessible** — record what you could and could not see; do not force access.
- **Staircase and common areas** — relevant especially for flats and apartments.
- **Terrace, roof, and open areas** — where relevant and accessible.
- **Utilities and services** — water, electricity, road connectivity, where relevant.
- **Construction stage** — for under-construction property, one of the most important sets.
- **Visible defects or site issues** — cracks, dampness, an apparent deviation; capture it as it is.

For an **independent house**, capture every visible elevation, because the side and rear often tell a different story from the polished front. For a **flat**, the staircase, lift lobby, and common areas matter as much as the unit itself. For an **under-construction property**, the RCC frame stage — columns cast, slab laid, brickwork started — drives a large part of the report, so the stage has to be visible in the photos, not just described in a note.

#### Documents and location support photos

These support the file but are never proof on their own.

- **Documents shown at the site** — sale deed, sketch, tax receipt, approved plan, where available. Photograph what is actually presented.
- **GPS or map-camera photo** — where useful, a location-stamped image can help tie a photo to a place and time. Treat it as a **helpful reference only.** It does **not** prove ownership, identity, or genuineness, it does not detect fraud, and it does not make anything compliant — and it should never be presented as if it does.

#### Special-case photos: consent and caution

- **Person present** — only where the office or bank process specifically allows it, only with the person's consent, and with proper caution. This is **not** a default photo, and it should never be taken without permission. When in doubt, leave it out and follow the assignment instructions.

### Common site photo mistakes (and how to avoid them)

Most weak photo sets fail in the same predictable ways.

**Blurry photos** — taken in a hurry, useless at the desk. Glance at the screen before moving on.
**The same angle repeated** — lots of photos, little information. Move around the property.
**No wide shot** — close-ups with no context, so no one can tell where the photo sits.
**No sequence** — photos in random order, so the office cannot follow the property.
**Photos mixed from multiple properties** — the single most damaging mistake, because now nobody trusts which photo belongs to which file.
**Photos not tied to an assignment reference** — the photos exist, but no one can connect them to the right file.

Almost every one of these is solved by slowing down for thirty seconds and checking before leaving.

### Site engineer photo handover: sending photos back cleanly

Capturing well is half the job. Handing over well is the other half.

Send photos **grouped by assignment**, not as one big dump across several visits. **Name or categorise** them where possible — even a rough split into frontage, boundaries, interiors, documents helps enormously. Add a short **note on what is missing**, if anything. And keep one visit's photos **separate from another visit's**, so they never get mixed.

The goal is that the report preparer opens the photos and immediately knows what they are looking at. (How to organise and retrieve them properly after handover is the subject of the next article in this batch.)

### How the office and the valuer actually use these photos

When **office staff draft the report**, they rely on the photos to describe the property, select report-ready images, and confirm the field notes against what the photos show. Clear photos make this fast; messy photos make it slow and error-prone.

When the **valuer reviews**, the photos are how they cross-check the property against the report — confirming the description matches, the observations are supported, and nothing important is unclear before sign-off.

The goal is not to take more photos. The goal is to take photos that let the report preparer and the valuer understand the property **without guessing.**

## FAQs

**1. How many photos should a site engineer take during a valuation visit?**
There is no fixed number, and a high count is not the goal. Enough photos to let someone who never visited understand the property — frontage, access, surroundings, boundaries, interiors where accessible, documents, and anything that needs explaining. A smaller, clear, well-sequenced set beats a large blurry one.

**2. Are GPS or map-camera photos required, and do they prove the property is genuine?**
They are useful where the office finds them helpful, but they are not universally required, and they do not prove ownership, identity, or genuineness, do not detect fraud, and do not make a report compliant. Treat a location-stamped photo as a helpful reference for tying an image to a place and time — nothing more.

**3. Should the borrower or owner be photographed at the site?**
Only where the specific office or bank process allows it, and only with the person's consent. It is not a default requirement, and people should never be photographed without permission. When in doubt, follow the assignment instructions and your office's normal process.

**4. What makes a site photo useless for report preparation?**
Blurriness, the same angle repeated, no wide shot for context, no sequence, photos mixed from different properties, and photos not tied to an assignment. Any one of these turns a photo into something the desk cannot rely on.

**5. What is the right way for a site engineer to hand over photos?**
Grouped by assignment, categorised where possible, with a short note on anything missing, and kept separate from other visits. The aim is that the report preparer opens the set and immediately knows what each photo is.
