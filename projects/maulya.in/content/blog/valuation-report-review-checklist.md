A report that is typed is not the same as a report that is ready to leave the office.

The draft looks finished. Every field is filled, the figures are in, the photos are attached. The temptation is to treat "looks complete" as "ready to submit," especially when the due date is close. But a complete-looking draft and an approved report are two different states — and the gap between them is the review step that catches mistakes before they leave the office.

Skipping that step does not save time. It moves the review from inside the office to the branch, where a correction costs far more — a returned report, a re-submission, and a delay that lands on the next assignment too.

## "Draft ready" and "approved for submission" are not the same state

A draft is ready when the office has assembled everything it can. A report is approved when the valuer has reviewed it and is willing to put their name on it. The first is an office milestone; the second is a professional decision. Collapsing the two — letting a finished-looking draft go out without that decision — is where avoidable corrections come from.

It helps to make the two states explicit in how the office works. A report has a status, and "drafted" and "approved" are different statuses, with the valuer's review as the gate between them.

## What the valuer actually reviews

The review is not a re-typing or a second draft. It is a structured pass over the file, checking that what the report says matches what the file supports and what the valuer intends. Grouping it keeps it fast.

**Identity and assignment block.** Bank, branch, borrower and co-borrower, assignment reference — checked against the source, not against the previous report. This is where carry-forward errors hide, so it is worth a deliberate look even though it feels routine.

**Property and location.** Address, landmark, locality chain, property type and status — consistent through the report and matching the site that was actually visited.

**Site observations and evidence match.** The described property matches the photos; the documents listed match the images in the file; the observations in the report are the ones recorded on site. If the report says ground-plus-one, the photos should show ground-plus-one — not the plot next door.

**Valuation inputs and figures.** The rates or unit values, the area adopted, the approach, the adjustments and the final figures are reviewed as the valuer's professional decisions. Cross-check that the figures in the report match what the valuer intended and that the report is internally consistent. This part of the review cannot be delegated — it is the core of why the report carries a valuer's name.

**Carry-forward cleanup and corrections.** Old template remnants removed, every correction from the first read actually applied, assumptions and remarks reviewed and still appropriate for this property.

**Final version and storage.** The final PDF or print version is the one being submitted — not an earlier draft — the submission method and status are recorded, and the final file is stored where it can be retrieved later.

## Who does what — and where the line sits

A clean review depends on a clear split of responsibility.

**Office staff** assemble a complete draft, flag anything uncertain rather than papering over it, apply corrections accurately, and — the firm line — never send a report as final without the valuer's approval. Preparing the report is their job; approving it is not.

**The valuer** brings the professional judgment the report exists to express, performs the final review, and signs off. The valuer remains responsible for the professional content and the decision to submit. The review step is where that responsibility is exercised, not assumed.

This split is not about hierarchy. It is about putting each decision with the person accountable for it.

## Review before submission beats correction after the branch

There are only two places a problem in a report gets caught: inside the office before submission, or outside it afterward. The first is a quiet correction. The second is a returned report, a re-submission, an awkward follow-up, and a delay — and it quietly affects how seriously the office's work is taken next time.

A review step that happens before submission converts expensive external corrections into cheap internal ones. That is the entire case for it. It is not extra work; it is the same checking, done where it costs the least.

## A note on what this checklist is — and is not

This is an internal office workflow checklist. It is not a legal or compliance checklist, and it does not by itself satisfy any external requirement. Banks and offices have their own processes, and those vary; the valuer remains responsible for professional judgment and for whatever the specific assignment requires. The purpose here is narrower and practical: to make sure a report is internally consistent, supported by its own file, and actually reviewed before it carries the valuer's name out the door.

## Make approval a visible status, not a verbal nod

The most useful change most offices can make is to stop treating approval as an implicit, verbal step — "yeah, send it" — and start treating it as a visible status that a report either has or does not. When approval is visible, no one has to guess whether a report was reviewed; the workflow shows it.

The final review then answers one clean question: is this report ready to carry the valuer's name and be submitted? If the answer is not a clear yes, it is not ready — regardless of how finished the draft looks.

## Practical checklist

**Internal approval checklist** — before a report leaves the office.

**Identity & assignment**
- [ ] Bank and branch checked against the source
- [ ] Borrower / co-borrower names correct and consistent
- [ ] Assignment reference correct

**Property & location**
- [ ] Address, landmark, locality chain consistent through the report
- [ ] Property type and status correct

**Observations & evidence**
- [ ] Report description matches the photos
- [ ] Documents listed match the images in the file
- [ ] Site observations match what was recorded on site

**Valuation (valuer's judgment)**
- [ ] Rates / unit values as the valuer decided
- [ ] Area adopted confirmed
- [ ] Approach / method reviewed
- [ ] Figures cross-checked and internally consistent
- [ ] Adjustments / remarks reviewed

**Cleanup & corrections**
- [ ] Old template carry-forward removed
- [ ] All first-read corrections applied

**Final version & storage**
- [ ] Final PDF / print is the version actually being submitted
- [ ] Submission method and status recorded
- [ ] Final file stored for later retrieval

**Sign-off**
- [ ] Valuer has reviewed and approved
- [ ] Report status marked "approved," not just "drafted"

## FAQs

**What is the difference between a draft report and an approved report?**
A draft is ready when the office has assembled everything it can. A report is approved when the valuer has reviewed it and is willing to put their name on it. The first is an office milestone; the second is a professional decision. Letting a finished-looking draft go out without that decision is how avoidable corrections happen.

**Who should give final approval on a valuation report?**
The valuer. The professional content — rates, area, approach, figures, judgment — and the decision to submit sit with the valuer. Office staff prepare and flag, but approval is not theirs to give. That is the entire point of a final review step.

**Can office staff submit a report without valuer review?**
As an internal practice, they should not. Sending a report as final without the valuer's review skips the one step where professional judgment is confirmed and where carry-forward and matching errors are most likely to be caught. Preparing the report and approving it are two different responsibilities.

**Does a review checklist make the report legally compliant?**
No. This is an internal quality and consistency checklist, not a legal or compliance one, and it does not by itself satisfy any external requirement. Banks and offices have their own processes, which vary. The checklist's job is narrower: to make sure the report is internally consistent and actually reviewed before submission.

**When should the final review happen?**
Before submission — not after the branch sends the report back. A problem caught inside the office is a quiet correction; the same problem caught at the branch is a returned report, a re-submission, and a delay. The review exists to move that catch earlier, where it costs the least.
