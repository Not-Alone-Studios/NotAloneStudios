# How Site Engineers Can Submit Evidence Cleanly to Valuers

A site visit is not finished when the site engineer leaves the property. It is finished when the valuer can understand the visit without calling back.

This is a small shift in thinking, but it changes everything about how a field visit is handed over. "I went to the property" and "the office has everything it needs to prepare the report" are two completely different statements. The gap between them is where most report-preparation delays live.

A site engineer can do a thorough visit — reach the right property, take good photos, collect the documents, note the boundaries — and still hand it over so badly that the office spends an hour reconstructing what happened. The visit was fine. The handover wasn't.

This article is about that handover: what to submit, how to submit it, and what the people on the receiving end actually need.

## Why completing the visit and submitting evidence are not the same

In many offices, evidence arrives as a stream of WhatsApp images with no order and no note. The field person considers the job done the moment those images are sent. From their side, it is — they were at the site, they captured everything, they forwarded it.

But the people who use that evidence — the report preparer and the valuer — receive a pile of unlabelled images and no context. They don't know which photo is the front, whether the interior was seen, what's pending, or even, on a busy day, whether all these photos are from the same property.

So the visit gets "completed" several times over: once in the field, and again every time someone calls back to fill a gap. Clean submission is what collapses all of that into a single handover.

## Site Engineer Evidence Submission for Valuation: What a Clean Handover Includes

The exact set depends on the bank and the office's normal process, but a usable handover from a site visit commonly includes three kinds of material:

**Visual evidence**
- **Photographs** — the property, its front, the approach road, surroundings, and interior where accessible
- **Document images** — whatever documents were shown or collected at the site, captured clearly enough to read

**Observations and measurements**
- **Site notes** — a short account of what was observed
- **Measurements** — whatever dimensions were taken on site, as per the office's process
- **Occupancy status** — self-occupied, tenanted, vacant, or locked
- **Access and road observations** — how the property is approached and the condition of the access
- **Boundary observations** — what's on each side, as seen on the ground

**Status and logistics**
- **Pending items** — anything that couldn't be done or is still awaited
- **Who was present** — the contact met at the site
- **Visit date and time** — when the visit actually happened

The point of listing these isn't to turn a visit into paperwork. It's that every one of these, when missing, becomes a phone call later. The handover is simply all of these reaching the office together, in a form they can use.

## How to submit evidence cleanly

Having the right evidence isn't enough. How it's submitted decides whether it saves time or creates work.

### Group everything by assignment reference

Every file should travel under its assignment reference — the loan account, application number, or whatever the office uses to identify the case. Photos, documents, and notes for one property stay together under one reference. This single habit prevents most mix-ups.

### Never mix multiple property visits

If three properties were visited in a day, their evidence should never sit together in one undifferentiated batch. Separate them at the point of sending, not later. A photo from the wrong property reaching a report is one of the most serious errors in this work, and it almost always starts with visits not being kept apart.

### Label or categorise the photos

The office shouldn't have to guess which photo is the front, which is the road, which is the interior. Even a rough grouping — exterior, approach, interior, documents — makes the photos usable instead of a puzzle.

### Send readable document images

A document photo that can't be read is the same as no photo. Before sending, the field person should be able to actually read the survey number, the area, and the measurements on the image — not just see that a page was captured.

### Include a short visit summary

Two or three lines change the entire handover: what was done, what was seen, what's pending. *"Exterior and interior done. Boundaries confirmed on all sides. Owner will share approved plan tomorrow."* That sentence answers questions the office would otherwise have to call about.

### Flag pending items clearly

If something is incomplete — a document to follow, an interior to revisit, a measurement that couldn't be taken — it should be stated, not left for the office to discover at the desk. A pending item that's flagged is a known task. A pending item that's hidden is a delay waiting to happen.

### Send evidence soon after the visit

Memory of a visit fades fast, especially across several visits in a day. Evidence sent the same day, while the details are fresh, is far more reliable than evidence assembled from memory two days later.

## What the valuer needs from the handover

The valuer is going to review and sign off on the report, so what they need from the handover is specific:

- **Usable evidence** they can review clearly, not images they have to interpret
- **Clarity on gaps** — what's missing and why, stated openly
- **No hidden assumptions** — nothing that looks confirmed but isn't
- **Observations, not conclusions** — what the field person saw, not what they decided it means

That last point matters. The site engineer records that a portion appeared to be under construction, or that the property looked larger than the description. The valuer decides what that signifies. A handover that quietly slips in conclusions about approval, ownership, or legality creates risk; a handover that sticks to plain observation supports the valuer's judgment instead of replacing it.

## What office staff need from the handover

The report preparer has a different but overlapping set of needs:

- **Report-ready material** — evidence organised enough to drop into a draft without sorting it first
- **Clear document status** — what was collected, what's readable, what's still awaited
- **The ability to select photos** — a set wide enough and clear enough to choose from
- **The ability to ask targeted questions** — so that if a follow-up is needed, it's one specific question, not "can you explain this whole visit again?"

A clean handover doesn't eliminate follow-up entirely. But it changes follow-up from a reconstruction into a single precise question.

## Bad handover vs clean handover

Here's the difference in practice.

**A bad handover:** 38 photos forwarded on WhatsApp across two chats, no labels, no note. Three of them are actually from a different property visited the same morning. Two document images are too blurry to read. There's no mention that the interior was locked. The message says: *"Done sir."*

What happens next: the preparer calls to ask which photos are the front and the road. Calls again about the blurry documents. Discovers mid-draft that there are no interior photos and calls a third time to find out why. The report sits for two days while these get resolved.

**A clean handover:** the same visit, submitted under the assignment reference, photos grouped as exterior / approach / interior / documents, document images checked and readable, plus a note: *"Exterior and interior done. Boundaries confirmed on all four sides. Tenant occupied. Owner will send approved plan tomorrow — marked pending."*

What happens next: the preparer starts drafting immediately. The one open item — the approved plan — is already known and tracked. No reconstruction, no guessing, often no call at all.

Same visit. Same field person. Same effort. Completely different outcome — and the only difference is how it was handed over.

## Why this reduces calls and delay

Every gap in a handover becomes a phone call, and every phone call is a small delay multiplied across every file the office handles. A clean handover front-loads the few minutes it takes to organise evidence, and saves the many minutes — and the days of waiting — that gaps create downstream.

The test for a good handover is simple: can the valuer and the report preparer understand what happened at the site without calling back? If yes, the visit is genuinely finished. If no, it isn't — no matter how thorough the field work was.

## A clean evidence handover checklist

**Before you send anything**
- [ ] All evidence is under the correct assignment reference
- [ ] This property's photos and documents are separated from any other visits that day
- [ ] Photos are grouped or labelled (exterior, approach, interior, documents)
- [ ] Every document image is readable — survey number, area, measurements legible
- [ ] Occupancy status is stated
- [ ] Access/road and boundary observations are included, or noted as pending
- [ ] Pending items are listed explicitly
- [ ] A two-to-three line visit summary is attached
- [ ] Date and time of visit, and who was present, are noted
- [ ] Evidence is going out the same day, while details are fresh

**The handover passes if:** the valuer and report preparer could understand the visit from what you sent — without calling you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Isn't sending the photos enough?**
Not usually. Photos without grouping, document status, occupancy, and a short summary force the office to reconstruct the visit. Sending evidence and submitting a usable handover are different things.

**How should a site engineer handle several visits in one day?**
Keep each property's evidence separate at the point of sending, each under its own assignment reference. Mixing them is how a wrong-property photo ends up in a report.

**Does the site engineer decide anything about the property's value or legality?**
No. They record observations of what was visible. Valuation judgment, and any conclusion about approval, ownership, or legality, is the valuer's role.

**What's the single most useful thing to add to a handover?**
A two-to-three line summary of what was done, what was seen, and what's pending. It answers most of the questions the office would otherwise call about.

**How soon should evidence be submitted after a visit?**
As soon as practical, ideally the same day while details are fresh. Evidence assembled from memory days later is less reliable and more likely to have gaps.
