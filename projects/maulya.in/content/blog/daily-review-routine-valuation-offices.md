A valuation office does not need a long meeting every morning. It needs ten honest minutes with the pending list.

Not a meeting where people report what they did yesterday. Not a discussion that drifts into general complaints about borrowers. Ten minutes, standing if possible, going through the active files and asking one question of each: what needs to happen on this today, and who's doing it?

Most offices that lose control don't lose it because nobody works hard. They lose it because the active files are never looked at together, in one place, every day.

## Why a daily rhythm beats a good memory

When an office handles a handful of assignments at a time, memory is enough. You know where each file is because there aren't many to know.

The trouble starts as volume climbs. At thirty or forty active files, memory doesn't fail dramatically — it fails quietly. One borrower who needed a callback slips. One draft that was waiting on the valuer sits an extra three days. One submitted report never gets stored. None of these are disasters on their own. Together, over a month, they're the reason the office feels permanently behind.

A short daily review is one of the simplest ways to catch this slippage — before the branch or the borrower catches it for you.

## What ten minutes should actually cover

The review works best as a quick pass through the active files by stage. You're not solving each file; you're spotting which ones have stopped moving. A useful pass covers:

- **New assignments received** — anything come in that hasn't been actioned or assigned?
- **Site visits pending today or tomorrow** — are they confirmed, and is the borrower reachable?
- **Borrower or contact issues** — anyone not answering, anything to reschedule?
- **Evidence and photos not received** — engineer visited but nothing's in yet?
- **Documents pending** — what's awaited, and who's chasing it?
- **Valuation inputs pending** — anything blocked on a decision?
- **Report drafts pending** — what's mid-preparation?
- **Valuer review pending** — what's sitting with the valuer for sign-off?
- **Corrections pending** — what came back from review and hasn't been fixed?
- **Reports ready for submission** — what can go out today?
- **Submitted reports not stored** — what's done but not filed?
- **Overdue files** — anything that's been stuck longer than it should be?

You won't have something against every line every day. The value is in the scan itself — the act of looking, so nothing sits in a blind spot.

## Who needs to be there

Not the whole office, and not always the same people.

The **valuer** and the **office staff** who handle assignments are the core. The **site engineer** joins when the day's visits or pending evidence need sorting out — but they do not need to sit through report-review discussion. The point is a focused review, not a full-attendance ritual. If someone has nothing to contribute and nothing to take away, they don't need to be in the room.

## A simple structure for the ten minutes

A loose order keeps the review from wandering. One that works:

1. **New files** — what came in, who's taking it.
2. **Site visits** — today's and tomorrow's, and any borrower issues.
3. **Report preparation** — drafts in progress and what's blocking them.
4. **Valuer review** — what's awaiting sign-off or corrections.
5. **Follow-ups** — pending borrower, branch, document, and evidence threads.
6. **Closure and storage** — what's ready to submit, and what's submitted but not yet stored.

Go stage by stage. The structure does most of the work of keeping it short.

## Produce actions, not conversation

This is the part that separates a useful review from a daily complaint session.

The review is not for talking about work. It's for deciding the next move. Every file that comes up should leave the ten minutes with three things attached: an **owner**, a **next step**, and an **expected date**. "We're waiting on the borrower" is not an outcome. "Priya calls the borrower today, visit to be fixed by Thursday" is.

If an item leaves the review without those three things, it will be back tomorrow, unchanged — and the review will start to feel pointless, which is how these routines quietly die.

## Daily review vs weekly review

The two do different jobs, and it helps not to confuse them.

The **daily review** is about active files: what moves today, what's stuck, who acts. It's tactical and fast.

The **weekly review** is about patterns. Which stage keeps clogging — is it always the valuer-review step, or always borrower access? Which type of assignment keeps slipping? Is the office quietly taking on more work than it can clear? These are questions you can't see in a single day, and they don't belong in the ten-minute scan. A weekly look at the shape of the work keeps the daily routine from becoming a treadmill.

## Keep it from becoming bureaucratic

A word of caution, because this is where good intentions go wrong.

The moment the daily review turns into a formal status-reporting exercise — where people feel they have to defend their work or justify a delay — it's finished. People start managing the meeting instead of the files. Keep it short. Keep it standing. Let the pending list do the talking, not the personalities. The goal is simple and slightly unglamorous: to expose which files already need action, before someone outside the office reminds you.

If the review ever starts creating more work than it resolves, it has drifted from its purpose. Pull it back to ten honest minutes with the list.

## Practical Checklist: The 10-Minute Daily Review

Scan the active files for each of these:

- [ ] New assignments received (assigned to someone?)
- [ ] Site visits pending today / tomorrow (confirmed? borrower reachable?)
- [ ] Borrower or contact issues to resolve
- [ ] Evidence / photos not yet received
- [ ] Documents pending
- [ ] Valuation inputs pending
- [ ] Report drafts in progress
- [ ] Reports awaiting valuer review
- [ ] Corrections pending after review
- [ ] Reports ready for submission today
- [ ] Submitted reports not yet stored
- [ ] Overdue files stuck longer than they should be

**For every item that comes up:** owner + next step + expected date. As far as possible, every item should leave with these three.

## FAQs

**1. What is a daily review routine for a valuation office?**
It's a short daily pass — usually 10 to 15 minutes — through the office's active bank valuation assignments, checking which ones have stopped moving and assigning a clear next step for each. It's a control habit, not a meeting.

**2. How long should the daily review take?**
Around ten to fifteen minutes for most offices. If it routinely runs longer, it's probably drifting into discussion or pattern questions that belong in a weekly review instead.

**3. Who should attend?**
The valuer and the office staff who handle assignments form the core. The site engineer joins only when site visits or pending evidence need sorting. It's a focused review, not a full-attendance ritual.

**4. What's the difference between a daily and a weekly review?**
The daily review is tactical — what moves today, what's stuck, who acts. The weekly review is about patterns — which stage keeps clogging, which assignment types slip, whether the office is overloaded. Keep them separate so the daily one stays short.

**5. How do we stop the review from becoming bureaucratic?**
Keep it short and standing, let the pending list lead, and make sure every item leaves with an owner, a next step, and a date. The moment it becomes a status-reporting or blame exercise, it stops being useful.
