# Common Site Visit Mistakes That Delay Valuation Reports

A weak site visit rarely announces itself at the site. It announces itself later, when the report preparer starts asking questions.

The field person reached the property. They met someone. They took photos. They came back and said "done." On paper, the visit is complete. Then, two days later, the report is stuck — because the address on the photos doesn't match the assignment, the interior shots are missing, and nobody can read the survey number in the document photo.

This is the quiet pattern in a lot of valuation offices. The site visit is treated as finished the moment the field person leaves the property. But the visit isn't really finished until the office can prepare a report from what was brought back — without calling the field person three times.

This article is not about blaming site engineers. Most site visit mistakes in property valuation are not carelessness — they happen because nobody told the field person what the desk actually needs, or because the visit was rushed between two other visits the same day.

This is not another site visit checklist. It is a delay-prevention list — the mistakes that make a completed visit unusable at the desk. The point is to name each common mistake, show how it delays the report, and suggest a small discipline that prevents most of them.

## Why site visit mistakes surface so late

A site visit mistake is almost never caught at the site. It's caught at the desk.

When the report preparer sits down to draft, that's when the gaps show up. A photo that looked fine on a phone screen is unreadable when pasted into a report. A boundary that "everyone knows" was never written down. The owner who promised to share the approved plan "later" was never followed up.

By then, the field person may have moved on to other visits. The branch is calling to ask when the report will be ready. And the office is now spending time reconstructing a visit that already happened — instead of preparing the next report.

That delay is the real cost. Not the mistake itself, but the back-and-forth it creates afterward.

## 10 Site Visit Mistakes That Delay Property Valuation Reports

Here are the ones that show up again and again in valuation office work — and what each one quietly costs.

### Mistake 1: Leaving without confirming the contact and the landmark

The assignment usually comes with a borrower name, a phone number, and an address. The address is often incomplete or slightly off — a plot number without a clear lane, a locality without a landmark.

If the field person heads out without calling the contact first and confirming a recognisable landmark, the visit can start with twenty minutes of driving in circles. Worse, it can end at a property that looks plausible but isn't the right one.

**The delay:** a wrong-property visit may not be discovered until drafting — and then the entire visit has to be repeated.

### Mistake 2: Reaching the locality but not confirming the exact property

This is the more dangerous version of the first mistake, because it hides better.

The field person reaches the right area. There's a building that matches the rough description. Photos get taken. But the exact plot, the exact flat, the exact unit — that was never confirmed against the document or with the owner on site.

In layouts with similar-looking plots, or buildings with multiple similar units, this is easy to get wrong and hard to catch later. The photos look complete, so nobody questions them until something doesn't add up.

**The delay:** re-verification, sometimes a repeat visit, and a valuer who now doesn't fully trust the rest of the evidence.

### Mistake 3: Taking many photos, but none with context

A common assumption is that more photos means a safer visit. Forty photos come back. But they're forty close-ups of walls, floors, and corners — and not one of them shows which property this is, where it sits, or how it connects to the road.

Photos without context are hard to use. The report preparer can't tell the front of the building from the back, can't place the property in its surroundings, and can't confirm anything from the images alone.

**The delay:** the preparer often has to call and ask "which photo is the front?" — and the field person may not remember either.

### Mistake 4: Missing approach road and access photos

Many valuation offices like to show how the property is approached — the road leading to it, the width and condition of access, the immediate surroundings. It's a normal part of how a property is described.

Field people focused only on the building itself often skip this. They photograph the structure thoroughly and forget the road entirely.

**The delay:** the preparer realises there's nothing to describe the access with, and either the field person goes back or the report goes out thinner than it should.

### Mistake 5: Not recording boundaries as seen on site

Boundaries on documents and boundaries on the ground don't always match. A normal part of a site visit is noting what's actually there on each side — a road, a vacant plot, a neighbour's compound wall, a drain.

When the field person doesn't note the boundaries as observed, the office is left with only the document boundaries and no way to confirm them. That's a gap a careful valuer will want closed before signing.

**The delay:** a follow-up call or visit specifically to confirm what's on each side.

### Mistake 6: Not noting occupancy or "interior not accessible" status

Sometimes the property is locked. Sometimes it's tenanted and the tenant won't allow interior access. Sometimes only the exterior could be seen. These are all normal field situations.

The mistake is not noting it. The field person comes back, the office assumes interior photos exist, and only at drafting does someone realise there are none — with no explanation of why.

**The delay:** time lost figuring out whether the interior was missed by mistake or genuinely couldn't be accessed, and a possible repeat visit to find out.

### Mistake 7: Collecting document photos but not checking readability

Document photos are some of the most-used evidence in report preparation — the survey number, the area, the measurements, the owner details often come straight from them.

A blurry document photo is almost useless. But on a phone screen, blur is hard to notice. The field person captures the page, it looks fine in the moment, and the unreadability is only discovered when someone tries to actually read it at the desk.

**The delay:** the office has to ask for the document again, and if the owner isn't reachable, the report waits.

### Mistake 8: Not noting visible issues or mismatches as observations

A field person isn't there to make valuation judgments — that's the valuer's role. But they are the only person who actually saw the property. If something visible stands out — an extra floor that wasn't expected, a portion under construction, a property that looks different from the description — that's worth noting as a plain observation.

When these things aren't recorded, the valuer reviews the report without knowing what the field person saw with their own eyes. Important context simply disappears.

**The delay:** questions raised during review that could have been answered upfront, sending the file back into the field-and-office loop.

A note on wording here: the field person should record what they observed, not draw conclusions about legality, approval, or ownership. "A portion appeared to be under construction" is an observation. Deciding what that means is for the valuer.

### Mistake 9: Mixing photos from multiple properties

On a busy day, one field person may visit three or four properties. If the photos all sit together in one camera roll with no separation, they get mixed.

A photo from the wrong property slipping into a report is one of the more serious errors that can happen — and it usually traces back to a day when visits weren't kept separate.

**The delay:** at best, time spent untangling which photo belongs to which file. At worst, an error that may not be caught until much later.

### Mistake 10: Not sending a clear handover note to the office

Even when the photos and documents are fine, the visit can still stall if the field person sends everything with no summary.

No "interior was locked, exterior done." No "owner will share the approved plan tomorrow." No "boundaries confirmed on all four sides." Just a pile of images and the word "done."

The office is then forced to reconstruct the visit by guessing and calling. The single most useful thing a field person can send is a two-line summary of what was done and what's still pending.

**The delay:** every gap that a one-line note could have closed instead becomes a phone call.

## The pattern behind all ten

Notice that almost none of these are about effort. The field person in every case did the visit. The problem is that the visit produced evidence the desk couldn't fully use.

That's why "did you go?" is the wrong question. The right question is "can the office prepare the report from what you brought back, without calling you?"

## A small discipline that prevents most of this

You don't need a long checklist to fix most of these. You need two short habits — one before the visit, one right after.

**Before leaving (two minutes):** Call the contact, confirm a landmark, and re-read the assignment so you know which property you're confirming and what the office will need.

**Before sending evidence (two minutes):** Check that the property is identifiable in the photos, the document images are readable, the access and boundaries are covered or noted as pending, and there's a two-line summary of what was done and what's still open.

Four minutes of discipline at the right two moments prevents most of the delays above. Not because the field person works harder — but because the visit now ends in a form the office can actually move on.

## A field discipline checklist that prevents report delays

**Before leaving for the site**
- [ ] Called the contact and confirmed a recognisable landmark
- [ ] Re-read the assignment and noted which exact property or unit to confirm
- [ ] Noted anything specific the office needs for this particular file

**At the site**
- [ ] Confirmed the exact property against the document or with the owner
- [ ] Photographed the front, the approach road, and the surroundings — not only interiors
- [ ] Noted boundaries as seen on each side
- [ ] Recorded occupancy status (self-occupied / tenanted / vacant / locked)
- [ ] If interior wasn't accessible, noted why
- [ ] Captured document images and checked each one is readable before leaving
- [ ] Noted any visible point worth flagging — as an observation, not a conclusion

**Before sending evidence**
- [ ] Kept this property's photos separate from other visits the same day
- [ ] Confirmed the property is identifiable from the photos alone
- [ ] Listed any pending items (document to follow, interior to revisit, and so on)
- [ ] Wrote a two-line summary of what was done and what's still open

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the biggest site visit mistake in property valuation?**
The most costly one is taking photos and measurements before confirming the exact property. Reaching the right locality, or a building that matches the description, is not the same as confirming the exact plot or unit — against the document or with the owner on site. When that confirmation is skipped, everything captured afterward may belong to the wrong property, and the error often surfaces only at the desk, after the visit is over.

**Whose fault is it when a site visit causes a report delay?**
Usually nobody's, in the blame sense. Most delays come from the visit not producing desk-ready evidence — often because the field person wasn't told what report preparation needs, or the visit was rushed. The fix is clearer field discipline, not blame.

**How can a field person know the exact property if the address is incomplete?**
Call the contact before leaving, confirm a landmark, and verify the exact plot or unit on site against the document or with the owner. Reaching the right locality is not the same as confirming the right property.

**How many photos should be taken during a valuation site visit?**
There's no fixed number, and more isn't automatically better. What matters is coverage and context — the property identifiable, the approach and surroundings shown, boundaries and document images captured clearly.

**What should a field person do if the interior is locked or inaccessible?**
Note it clearly — that the interior could not be accessed, and why — and inform the office. The mistake is not the locked door; it's leaving without recording that the interior wasn't seen.

**Can a site engineer note problems they see at the property?**
Yes, as plain observations of what was visible. They should not draw conclusions about legality, approval, or ownership — that's the valuer's judgment. "A portion appeared under construction" is fine; deciding what it means is not their role.
