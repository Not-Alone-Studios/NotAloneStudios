The first SOP in a valuation firm should not be a 40-page manual. It should be a one-page answer to one question: how does a file move?

Most small valuation offices that decide to "get organised" make the same mistake. They sit down to write a proper SOP, open a blank document, and try to cover everything — conduct, formats, compliance, escalation, designations. Forty pages later, the document is impressive, complete, and never opened again. On a busy day, nobody reads a manual. They do what they always did and call the valuer when stuck.

A first valuation firm SOP earns its place only if staff actually follow it when the office is busy. That means it has to be short, obvious, and built around the one thing every office already revolves around: the assignment.

## Why small offices need a simple SOP, not a big manual

Large institutions write large SOPs because they have departments, handovers, and people who may never meet. A small valuation office is the opposite — a handful of people who work side by side, plus site engineers on the road. They don't need a policy library. They need a shared answer to "what happens next" so they stop interrupting each other.

The test of a good first SOP is not how professional it looks. It is whether a new junior, on day three, can look at it and know what to do with a file that just came in — without asking the valuer.

## Valuation Firm SOP: Start With the Assignment Lifecycle

Don't organise your first SOP by topic. Organise it by the journey of a single bank valuation assignment, from the moment it arrives to the moment it is filed away. That journey already exists in your office. You are just making it visible.

A practical lifecycle for most offices looks like this:

1. Assignment received
2. Intake details recorded
3. Site visit assigned
4. Pre-visit preparation
5. Evidence, photos, and documents handed over
6. Valuation inputs from the valuer
7. Report draft prepared
8. Valuer review and corrections
9. Final approval
10. Submission and storage

That's the spine. Everything else is detail hung on it.

## For each step, answer four questions

This is where most SOPs are too vague to be useful. For every stage above, your one-pager should answer four short questions:

- **Who owns this step?** One name or one role. Not "the team."
- **What must be captured here?** The specific inputs or files this step produces.
- **What blocks it from moving forward?** What must be complete before the file advances.
- **What gets marked pending?** So an incomplete file is visibly waiting, not silently stuck.

Take the **intake** step as an example. *Owner:* office staff. *Captured:* branch name, assignment reference, applicant/contact details, property location, purpose, any documents shared, date received. *Blocks progress:* a file with no contact detail and no property location should not move to site-visit scheduling. *Marked pending:* "awaiting documents from branch."

Do that for each of the ten steps and you have a real SOP — not because it is long, but because every step has an owner, a definition of done, and a way to be visibly incomplete.

## Separate the four kinds of responsibility

A good valuation SOP keeps four things clearly apart, because mixing them is where quality slips:

- **Office staff tasks** — receiving, recording, coordinating, organising documents, assembling drafts, storing.
- **Site engineer tasks** — visiting, measuring, photographing, capturing site conditions, handing over evidence cleanly.
- **Valuer judgment** — rates, methods, assumptions, the actual valuation.
- **Final approval** — the valuer's review and sign-off before anything leaves the office.

The SOP should make it impossible to confuse "I collected the information" with "I decided the value." Staff and site engineers feed the file. The valuer decides and signs. Keeping that line visible protects both the work and the people doing it.

## How to keep the SOP practical

A first SOP survives only if it stays usable. A few rules that keep it alive:

- **Start at one page.** If it doesn't fit, you're writing a manual, not an SOP.
- **Use status names everyone says out loud.** "Awaiting review," "corrections pending," "submitted." The SOP and the daily conversation should use the same words.
- **Attach a small checklist per step**, not paragraphs of instruction. People follow checkboxes; they skim prose.
- **Review it weekly for a few minutes.** What got stuck this week, and at which step?
- **Change it only when a problem repeats.** Don't rewrite the SOP for a one-off. Fix what breaks twice.

The point is a living document that reflects how your office actually works, not an aspirational version of an office you don't have.

## Common SOP mistakes to avoid

Most failed SOPs fail for predictable reasons:

- **Too long.** Nobody reads it, so nobody follows it.
- **Copied from a generic template.** A general "process document" doesn't know what a site engineer hands over or what intake must capture. Yours must.
- **No owner per step.** If everyone owns a step, no one does.
- **No pending status.** Without a way to mark a file as visibly waiting, stuck files just disappear until a branch calls.
- **No final storage step.** The SOP ends at "submitted" and the finished report scatters into folders and chats — which becomes its own problem later.

If your first SOP avoids these five, it is already more useful than most.

## Start with the file, not the firm

You don't need to define your whole firm to write your first SOP. You need to follow one file honestly, from the branch's email to the stored final report, and write down what actually happens at each step — who touches it, what they produce, and what holds it up.

Do that, and the SOP stops being a document you wrote for the shelf. It becomes the answer your office gives, every day, to the only operational question that really matters: *where is this file, and what does it need next?*

## FAQs

**How long should the first SOP be?**
One page. If it doesn't fit on a page, you're writing a manual, and manuals don't get followed on busy days. Depth can come later, once the basic flow is genuinely in use.

**Who should write the SOP — the valuer or the staff?**
Best done together. The valuer knows where judgment must sit and where reviews are non-negotiable; the staff know the messy reality of how files actually move. An SOP written by one without the other usually misses something the other sees daily.

**Should the SOP follow a specific bank format?**
The SOP is about how *your office* moves a file, not about any bank's report format. Keep it focused on the internal workflow — intake, visit, evidence, inputs, draft, review, approval, storage — and let report formats remain a separate matter handled at the drafting and review steps, depending on the bank or office process.

**How often should we update the SOP?**
Glance at it weekly for a few minutes — what got stuck and where — but only *change* it when a problem repeats. Rewriting it for every one-off makes it unstable and erodes trust in it.

**What if staff don't follow the SOP?**
Usually that means the SOP is too long, too vague, or doesn't match how work really flows. If people quietly route around it, treat that as feedback about the document, not just about the people. A one-page SOP with clear owners and status names is far harder to ignore than a manual.
