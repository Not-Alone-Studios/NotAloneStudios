A valuation report is not delayed only when typing is slow. It is delayed when the person preparing it does not have the inputs needed to draft without guessing.

Walk into a valuation office on a busy afternoon and the bottleneck is rarely the keyboard. A report sits half-finished in a Word template, the cursor parked in the "built-up area" field, and the person preparing it is not typing at all — they are scrolling through WhatsApp trying to find the one message where the site engineer mentioned a measurement. That message is buried somewhere between a payment screenshot and a photo of an entirely different property from another assignment.

This is the part most people miss about report preparation. A report is not written from imagination. It is assembled from inputs. When the inputs are complete and sitting in one place, even an average typist finishes quickly. When the inputs are scattered across chats, phone calls, and someone's memory, even a fast typist slows to a crawl — and worse, starts guessing.

## A report is assembled, not written

Most offices treat the report as a writing task. It is closer to an assembly task. The valuer's judgment is the skilled part; everything around it is collecting the right pieces and dropping them into the right slots.

If you separate the report into the inputs it draws from, something useful happens. You can check whether a file is ready to draft *before* opening the template — instead of discovering a missing piece on the third paragraph, stopping, making a call, waiting, and coming back cold. The check takes a minute. The interruption it prevents takes far longer.

## Property Valuation Report Inputs: The Main Buckets to Check

Broadly, a property valuation report pulls from a few distinct buckets. Keeping them separate — in your head and in your file — is what makes a file "draftable."

### Assignment-level inputs

These arrive when the assignment is received: the bank and branch, the borrower (and co-borrower where applicable), an assignment reference or file number, the purpose of valuation where the bank shares it, and the due date or expected timeline. They sound trivial. They are also the first things a branch checks and the first things that get copied wrong from a similar recent file.

### Party and contact details

Borrower name spelling, co-applicant names, the contact person for the site visit, and any reference number the branch uses. Small inconsistencies here — a name spelled two ways, a co-borrower added in a later message and never recorded — quietly create mismatches between the file, the report header, and the bank's records.

### Property and location details

Where the property actually is: address, nearest landmark, locality or village, taluk, district and state, property type (residential plot, independent house, apartment, commercial unit, vacant land, and so on), and observations on access and approach road. In many parts of India, the postal address alone is not enough to locate a property — the locality chain and a landmark are what let the next person find it again. Boundaries as seen on site and occupancy status (self-occupied, tenanted, vacant, under construction) belong here too, recorded as observations.

### Site and building observations

This is what the site visit produces: land area details, built-up / plinth / carpet area where relevant to the property and the office's normal process, number of floors, construction type, age and condition as observed, the stage of construction where the property is incomplete, and any visible issues noted as observations. The wording matters — these are observations recorded by the person on site, not technical or legal conclusions.

### Evidence — photos and document images

Site photographs, images of the documents provided, and location support such as a map reference where it helps. Just as important is knowing what evidence is *missing or pending*, so the report is not quietly built around a gap nobody flagged.

### Valuer inputs — the part office staff must never guess

Here the line has to be firm. The rates or unit values applied, the approach or method chosen, confirmation of the area to be adopted, any adjustments or remarks, and the final valuation figures come from the valuer's professional judgment.

Office staff can prepare everything around these inputs, but they should not invent them. If the valuer has not yet provided a rate, the correct action is to mark it pending — not to carry forward last week's number because the property "looks similar." The valuer remains responsible for these professional decisions, and a report that fills them in by assumption is a report waiting to be corrected.

### Review comments and corrections

Finally, the file accumulates review notes — items to confirm, things to change, corrections after a first read. Treating these as part of the input set, rather than as scattered verbal instructions, is what keeps version two from quietly reintroducing the mistakes of version one.

## Why guessing a missing input is the most expensive shortcut

When an input is missing, there are two honest options: get it, or mark it pending. Guessing is the third option, and it is the one that costs the most.

A guessed built-up area becomes a figure the valuer has to catch and correct. A borrower name carried over from a previous file becomes a report the branch sends back. An assumed property type changes the entire description. Each guess that slips through turns into a correction cycle — a call, a re-type, a re-check, a re-send — and correction cycles are where turnaround time quietly disappears. The office feels busy, but a large share of that effort is redoing work that was never right the first time.

The uncomfortable part is that most of these guesses are made not out of carelessness but out of momentum. The report is due, the field is blank, and stopping feels slower than filling it. A simple input check removes that pressure, because the gap is visible before drafting starts, not discovered midway.

Consider a routine file — an independent house in a taluk town, home-loan purpose. On paper it looks simple. But the site engineer noted the built-up area in a voice note, the borrower's name on the bank email reads slightly differently from the name on the document scan, two of the eight photos are of the neighbouring plot, and the valuer confirmed the rate verbally but never in writing. None of these is a disaster on its own. Together, they are four small gaps that will surface one by one during drafting — each one stopping the work, each one needing a call. A file that looked "ready" was actually four interruptions away from done.

## A one-minute pre-drafting input check

Before opening the template, the question is not "can I start typing?" It is "does this file have enough to prepare the report without assumptions, and what is still missing?"

Input completeness is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a report that flows from a complete file and a report that is reverse-engineered from memory under deadline. The offices that draft fastest are rarely the ones that type fastest — they are the ones whose files are ready before anyone starts.

## Practical checklist

**Pre-drafting input check** — run before opening the report template.

**Assignment & parties**
- [ ] Bank and branch recorded correctly
- [ ] Borrower and co-borrower names confirmed and spelled consistently across the file and the documents
- [ ] Assignment reference / file number captured
- [ ] Purpose of valuation noted where the bank has shared it
- [ ] Due date / expected timeline known

**Property & location**
- [ ] Full address with nearest landmark
- [ ] Locality / village, taluk, district, state
- [ ] Property type confirmed
- [ ] Access / approach road observation noted
- [ ] Boundaries as seen recorded
- [ ] Occupancy status noted

**Site & building**
- [ ] Land area details available
- [ ] Built-up / plinth / carpet area where relevant to the property and office process
- [ ] Number of floors
- [ ] Construction type
- [ ] Age / condition as observed
- [ ] Construction stage where the property is incomplete
- [ ] Visible issues recorded as observations

**Evidence**
- [ ] Site photos present and clearly belonging to this property
- [ ] Document images included and readable
- [ ] Location support where useful
- [ ] Missing / pending evidence flagged

**Valuer inputs**
- [ ] Rate / unit value provided by the valuer (not assumed)
- [ ] Area to be adopted confirmed by the valuer
- [ ] Approach / method as decided by the valuer
- [ ] Adjustments / remarks where applicable
- [ ] Final valuation figures provided

**Before you start typing**
- [ ] Every "pending" item is visibly marked, not silently skipped
- [ ] Nothing in the valuer-inputs section is a guess

## FAQs

**What is the minimum information needed before starting a valuation report?**
At a practical level, you need enough to fill the report without guessing: confirmed assignment and party details, the property's location and type, the site observations and measurements, the relevant photos and document images, and the valuer's inputs. The exact set varies depending on the bank or office process and the property, but the test is simple — if a field would have to be filled by assumption, the input for it is missing.

**Should office staff decide the valuation rate if the valuer has not given it?**
No. Rates, the approach, the area to be adopted, and the final figures are professional decisions that sit with the valuer. If they are pending, the right step is to flag them and wait, not to carry forward a number from a similar file. Guessing here is the single most common way a report ends up needing correction.

**What is the difference between assignment details and property details?**
Assignment details are about the work — bank, branch, borrower, reference, due date. Property details are about the asset — address, type, area, observations. They come from different sources and get checked at different points, so keeping them as separate buckets makes both easier to confirm.

**What should happen if some inputs are missing at drafting time?**
Mark them clearly as pending and decide whether the report can be partly prepared around them or should wait. The mistake is the silent gap — a blank field filled with an assumption that no one flagged. A visible "pending" is recoverable; an invisible guess usually is not until the branch finds it.

**Is built-up area or carpet area needed for the report?**
It depends on the property and the office's normal process. Different property types and formats call for different area details, so rather than treating any single area type as always required, confirm which area the valuer intends to adopt for this property — and record it clearly so it is not re-derived or assumed later.
